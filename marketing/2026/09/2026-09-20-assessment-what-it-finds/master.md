Most network assessments get sold as discovery and delivered as a slide deck.

Here is what two weeks of actually looking turns up, nearly every time:

— Configurations that diverged from the standard years ago, in ways nobody chose
— Three tools holding three different answers to "how many devices do we have"
— A handful of changes that only one person knows how to make safely
— Documentation describing a network that was decommissioned

None of that is exotic. That is the point. The findings are almost never a clever architectural flaw — they are ordinary decisions, made under deadline, that nobody had reason to revisit.

The finding that surprises people most is the third one. Changes only one person can make safely never appear on a risk register, because nothing is broken and nothing is failing. It is the item that turns a resignation into an incident, and it is invisible until someone goes looking for it.

What makes an assessment worth paying for is not the list. It is the ordering: which of those actually costs you engineering hours every month, which is a real audit exposure, and which is ugly but harmless. Plenty of things on that list can be left alone for another year.

We do this before proposing any automation work, because automating a process nobody has looked at just makes the wrong thing faster.

If you already suspect what the first finding would be, you probably don't need a discovery phase — you need someone to scope the fix.
