# LinkedIn — your-side-of-the-engagement


The question nobody asks before an automation project is how many hours it will cost their own team.

Everyone sizes the vendor's effort. That part is a proposal with a number on it. What rarely gets sized is the client-side work, and that is where most delays actually come from.

Four things, specifically.

Access. Credentials, a jump host, firewall rules, and a decision about what privilege the automation runs with. In most engagements this takes longer than anything technical, because it crosses a security boundary and the approver has other priorities.

Decisions. A standard has to be agreed before it can be enforced. Which image version, which exception is legitimate, what happens to the sites that do not fit. These are not questions we can answer for you, and a draft somebody can argue with moves faster than a blank form.

Review. If the work is going to be trusted, your engineers have to read it. That is real hours from the busiest people in the organization, and it is not optional: automation nobody reviewed is automation nobody will run unattended.

Acceptance. Somebody has to say it is done, against criteria agreed up front, and that person has to be allowed to say no.

None of this is unusual and none of it is a complaint. It is simply the half of the plan that tends to be missing when a timeline slips and everyone is surprised.

So we put it in the proposal, with hours attached. A project needing twenty hours from your team is a different decision than one needing eighty, and you should make that call with the real number in front of you.

The timeline that slips is usually the one where only one side was planned.
