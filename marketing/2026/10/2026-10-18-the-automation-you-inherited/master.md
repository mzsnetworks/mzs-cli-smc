Most automation projects do not start from nothing. They start from something somebody already built.

A set of scripts from an engineer who has since left. A pipeline that half-works and gets run by hand. A tool that was bought, configured for two sites, and quietly abandoned. None of it is documented, and nobody will say out loud whether it can be trusted.

That is the normal starting condition, and it is harder than a blank page.

Harder because the first question is not technical. It is whether the thing is load-bearing. A script nobody runs is easy. A script somebody runs on the first Tuesday of the month, that four people depend on without knowing it, is a different problem, and the person who could tell you is the one who left.

So we read it before replacing it. Inherited automation usually encodes decisions that were correct, and the reason is rarely written down: a wait that exists because a platform needs it, an exclusion that looks arbitrary and prevents an outage. Rewriting from scratch discards those and finds them again the expensive way.

Then it is one decision per component. Keep it, and make it runnable by somebody other than its author. Rebuild it, because it is doing a real job badly. Or delete it, which applies to more of it than anyone expects.

The deliverable is not a larger framework. It is a smaller set of things that work, that your team can run, and that nobody is afraid of.

Automation nobody trusts is not a failed project. It is an inventory problem, and it is where we usually start.
