# LinkedIn — sold-on-speed


Wi-Fi 7 will be sold on speed and bought for capacity, and most enterprises will get neither.

The headline numbers are real in the sense that a lab can produce them. Wider channels, more spatial streams, the ability for a client to use two bands at once. None of it is marketing invention.

What it is, is the ceiling. And almost nobody in an enterprise is anywhere near their ceiling.

The constraint in a typical office is not the radio. It is the uplink from the access point, which is often still a gigabit port into a switch that was installed with the last refresh. A faster radio behind a saturated uplink produces a faster queue.

The second constraint is placement. Access points get mounted where there is cable and where the ceiling tile is convenient, which is rarely where the density is. Wider channels make placement matter more, not less, because channel reuse gets harder as channels get wider. In a dense deployment the new standard can perform worse than the old one if the design is unchanged.

The third is the client mix. The benefits arrive when both ends support them, and the device population in most enterprises spans about eight years. The barcode scanner from 2017 is not going to negotiate anything interesting.

None of this is an argument against upgrading. It is an argument about sequencing, and the order is unglamorous: uplinks and switching first, then a survey that reflects how the space is actually used, then radios.

Buy Wi-Fi 7 when the thing limiting you is the radio. If you cannot say with confidence that it is, the money buys a better ceiling above the same floor.
