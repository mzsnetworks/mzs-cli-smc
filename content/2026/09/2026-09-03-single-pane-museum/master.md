---

Your single pane of glass is a museum.

Beautiful. Curated. Everything in one place, labeled, behind glass. And like a museum, you can look at all of it and touch none of it.

I've sat in the demos. The pane is genuinely impressive — every system, one login, colour-coded. Then the alert fires, and watch what actually happens next.

Somebody sees red on the pane. They leave the pane. They open a terminal. They open the ticket system to find out whether anyone changed anything. They open the vendor portal because the device is under support. They message a colleague to ask what the pane means by "degraded."

The consolidation happened at the display layer. The friction was never in the display layer.

Dashboards show state. Work changes state. Those are different activities, and putting the first one behind a single login doesn't shorten the second one at all.

So the question I'd ask instead of "can I see everything from one place" is: how many tools does it take to get from noticing to fixing?

That number is measurable this afternoon. Take your last three incidents and count the systems somebody had to touch between the alert firing and the problem being fixed. Not the systems that displayed something — the systems somebody had to log into and act in.

What I'd rather have than a bigger pane:

→ An alert that arrives with its context already attached — what changed recently, what state the thing is in, who owns it. Most of the click-path exists to answer questions the alert could have answered itself.

→ A path from alert to action that doesn't cross an authentication boundary three times.

→ One place where work happens, even if state is displayed in several. Consolidating where you *act* is worth far more than consolidating where you *look*.

A dashboard that shows everything and changes nothing is a museum with very good lighting.

How many tools sit between your alert and your fix?
