---

The fastest way to stop the phone ringing during an incident is four lines of text.

Here's the loop I've watched play out more times than I'd like. The technical work competes with the communication work. The communication work always loses, because it feels like the part that isn't fixing anything. So updates stop going out. So people start calling to find out what's happening. So the technical work gets slower. So the updates fall further behind.

The people calling you aren't impatient. They're uninformed, and calling is the only lever they have.

Four lines, sent on a clock:

What's broken — in the language of the person affected, not the device. "Users at the Denver site can't reach the ERP," not "core-sw02 is flapping." Nobody outside the team can act on a hostname.

Who's affected — the scope you have actually confirmed. If you don't know the full blast radius yet, say what you've confirmed and say that you're still confirming.

What we're doing right now — one sentence, present tense. Not the plan for the next three hours. The thing happening at this moment.

When you'll hear from us next — an actual clock time. This is the line that stops the calls, and it's the only line that does.

What stays out: speculation about cause, an ETA you can't support, and anything you'll have to retract. During an incident, a cause you haven't proven isn't analysis. It's a rumor with your name on it, and it will outrun the correction.

The fourth line is the hard one, because it commits you. Send it anyway — and then send the next update on time even when it says nothing has changed. An on-time "no change" buys more trust than an early guess, every time.

None of this makes the outage shorter. It makes it quieter — which is often the same thing, because the interruptions were part of what slowed you down.

Four lines. On a clock. With no guesses in them.
