# LinkedIn — fewer-certainties


I used to think seniority was knowing more. It's mostly needing less.

Not less knowledge — less certainty. The senior engineers I learned the most from didn't have more facts loaded than everyone else in the room. They needed fewer of them before they'd move.

That looked like arrogance to me at first. It isn't. It's a different skill, and it has three parts.

The first is knowing which unknowns actually block the decision. A junior instinct — mine, for years — is to gather everything before acting, because everything feels equally load-bearing. It isn't. On most problems there are one or two facts that change what you'd do, and a dozen more that are merely interesting. Seniority is telling those apart quickly, getting the one or two, and moving.

The second is the inverse, and it's the part people skip. Knowing when you genuinely cannot proceed — and saying so out loud instead of producing a confident answer to fill the silence. "I don't have enough to call this yet, and here's specifically what I need" is a senior sentence. It's also much harder to say than a guess, because a guess sounds like competence and the truth sounds like hesitation.

The third is being wrong quickly and in public. A wrong hypothesis you say out loud gets corrected in ninety seconds by whoever knows better. The same hypothesis held privately steers your next two hours and nobody gets the chance to stop it. The cost difference is enormous, and the only price is looking briefly unsure in front of people.

None of this is about caring less. The stakes don't drop. What drops is the amount of certainty you require before you're willing to act on your read of the situation — and the amount of time you spend collecting facts that were never going to change the answer.

Seniority isn't more certainty. It's needing less of it, and knowing exactly which certainty you still need.

What's the one fact you'd want before making your next hard call?
