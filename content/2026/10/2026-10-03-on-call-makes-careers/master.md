On-call is where careers are actually made, and almost nobody says so out loud.

We describe it as a tax. Something you endure, negotiate down, or escape by getting senior enough. All of that is fair. The sleep cost is real, and a rotation that's genuinely brutal should be fixed rather than romanticized.

But it's also the single fastest place engineering judgment gets built. Both things are true, and we only ever say the first one.

Here's why it works, when it works.

You get the whole system, not your slice. Day to day you're inside your area. At 2am you're handed something that spans four of them and you have to reason across the boundaries, quickly, with the actual dependencies rather than the diagram of them. Nothing else in the job teaches the shape of the environment that fast.

You learn what actually breaks, as opposed to what's supposed to. Every environment has a gap between documented design and real behavior. On-call is where you meet it, repeatedly, with consequences.

And you learn to act with incomplete information under time pressure, then live with the result. That specific loop — decide, watch, be wrong sometimes, adjust — is what separates engineers who have opinions from engineers whose opinions are worth something.

The catch is that none of it is automatic. A rotation only teaches if the pages are real, the runbooks exist, somebody senior is reachable without shame, and the fixes from last month actually got made. A rotation where the same alert fires nightly and nothing is ever fixed doesn't build judgment. It builds resentment, and then it builds a resignation.

So if you run one: the difference between a rotation that develops people and one that burns them is whether the work it surfaces gets funded. That's a management decision, not an engineering one.

And if you're on one right now, at an hour you didn't choose: you're not just covering. You're building the thing that makes you hard to replace.

What did on-call teach you that nothing else would have?
