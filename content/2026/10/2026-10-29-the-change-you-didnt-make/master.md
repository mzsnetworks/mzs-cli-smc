The change you did not make is also a decision. It just never enters the record.

Change management is built to capture things that happen. Someone proposes, someone reviews, someone approves, it gets logged. Deciding to leave something alone produces none of that. No ticket, no approver, no artifact.

Which means the deferrals are invisible to every process we have.

I do not mean recklessness. Most of these are good calls at the time. We are not upgrading that firmware this quarter, because the change window is full and the risk right now is low. We are not replacing that switch yet, because the budget is next year. We are not enabling that feature until someone has time to test it properly. Every one defensible, and I have made all three.

The problem is what happens afterward. A change that goes ahead gets reviewed, documented, and revisited when it misbehaves. A change that does not go ahead gets discussed once, in a meeting, and then it stops existing. The reasoning evaporates. The condition it left behind does not.

Two years later somebody inherits that firmware version and has no idea it was a choice. It reads as neglect, or as how things have always been. Nobody reviews it, because from the outside there is nothing to review.

What helps is small. When you decide not to do something that mattered enough to discuss, write it down where the work lives: what you are not doing, why, and what would change your mind. That last part is the one that does the work — "we will revisit if the vendor issues a security advisory" is a trigger, and a trigger can fire. "Not now" cannot.

You are not creating more process. You are giving a decision that already happened somewhere to be found.

Because the worst inherited environments are not the ones where people made bad decisions. They are the ones where nobody can tell which conditions were decided at all.
