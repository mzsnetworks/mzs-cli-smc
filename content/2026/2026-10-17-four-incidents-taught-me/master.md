Four incidents taught me more than any certification I have sat, and it took me years to work out why.

A certification teaches you what is true. An incident teaches you what you do when you are not sure, which turns out to be most of the job.

The first one taught me to write down the time. I spent an afternoon chasing a fault that had started before the change everyone blamed, and nobody could prove it either way, because no one had recorded when it actually began. Now the first line of any incident note I keep is a timestamp of the last known good.

The second taught me that "we restarted it and it came back" is not a conclusion. It closed, it reopened four days later, and the second time cost far more than the first would have. If I cannot say why it broke, I now say that out loud rather than letting a working system imply that somebody knows.

The third taught me to check the thing I was certain about. The certainty was the reason I had not checked it, and it was wrong, and three hours went somewhere else. The check would have taken ninety seconds. I still do not enjoy this one.

The fourth taught me that the handover is the risk. A clean, technically correct fix, undone at 6am because the incoming engineer did not know a temporary change was still in place. Nobody made a mistake. The information simply did not travel.

What links them is that none of them changed a fact I knew. They each changed a default I run on: record the time, do not close what you cannot explain, verify the certainty, hand over the state.

Facts you can look up in the moment. Defaults are what you have when there is no moment to look anything up.

That is what the certification could not give me, and what four bad nights did.
