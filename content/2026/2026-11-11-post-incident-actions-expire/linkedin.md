# LinkedIn — post-incident-actions-expire


Post-incident actions expire, and almost nobody notices.

Read any mature postmortem and the findings are good. The timeline is honest, the contributing factors are named, and at the bottom there is a list of actions. Add monitoring here. Document that. Review the other thing quarterly.

Come back in six months and ask what happened to them. Usually one got done, because somebody was annoyed enough to do it that week. The rest aged out quietly.

This is not a discipline failure, and it is not laziness. It is that the list was written at the one moment nobody could say no to it.

An outage creates a short window where remediation is unarguable. Everybody in the room agrees it should be fixed, and that agreement costs nothing, because the work has no date, no owner and no budget attached yet. The action item is a statement of regret rather than a plan.

Two weeks later the regret has faded and the work still has no date, no owner and no budget. It now competes with the roadmap on equal terms, and it loses, because the thing that justified it is no longer in anybody's short-term memory.

So the test we apply is deliberately blunt. If an action does not have a name, a date, and a place in somebody's actual workload, it is not an action. It is a feeling somebody had on a bad day, written down.

Half as many actions with those three attached beats a list of fifteen that nobody owns. The fifteen are not a plan. They are an accurate record of how everyone felt at 3am.

And the uncomfortable part: the actions that die quietly are usually the ones that would have prevented the repeat.

How many actions from your last postmortem have a date on them?
