# Instagram — cheapest-bid-loses-decade

The cheapest bid usually wins the project and loses the decade. 💰

We're not arguing for expensive.

We're arguing that the number on the comparison sheet is the wrong number — and everybody involved knows it by year three. ⚙️

What a low bid concretely buys you:

→ fewer discovery hours, so the surprises arrive later as change orders
→ a team you haven't met, working on three other things
→ no line item for documentation — easiest thing to cut, hardest thing to notice missing
→ no knowledge transfer, so the environment leaves with the people who built it
→ equipment chosen on unit price rather than support model and lifecycle
→ a scope written tightly enough that anything real is outside it

None of that is dishonest. It's a rational response to being compared on one figure.

The costs are real. They just land later, and on a different budget — usually on the operations team, who never saw the bid and get to explain for eight years why a change takes three weeks.

The number worth comparing is cost per year of ownership across the expected life:

→ purchase
→ plus support
→ plus the operational hours it will consume
→ plus what it costs to change when the business changes

That last one is what nobody prices and everybody pays.

Two questions separate bids faster than anything else we know:

1️⃣ What did you assume that, if wrong, becomes a change order?

2️⃣ Who specifically is doing this work, and what else are they on?

Both are uncomfortable to answer honestly. That's the point — the discomfort is the information.

And plainly: we lose bids on price. This isn't a complaint about that. Buyers optimize the number they were handed. The fix is for the request to ask for the figure that includes the decade.

You don't buy a network once. You buy it every year for ten years.

When did you last find out the cheap option wasn't? 👇
