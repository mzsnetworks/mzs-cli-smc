## Thread (alternate)

1/ The cheapest bid usually wins the project and loses the decade. 🧵

2/ We're not arguing for expensive. We're arguing the number on the comparison sheet is the wrong number — and everybody knows it by year three.

3/ What a low bid concretely buys: fewer discovery hours (surprises arrive later as change orders), no documentation line, no knowledge transfer, equipment chosen on unit price, and a scope tight enough that anything real is outside it.

4/ None of that is dishonest. It's a rational response to being compared on one figure.

5/ The costs land later and on a different budget — usually on the operations team, who never saw the bid and spend eight years explaining why a change takes three weeks.

6/ Compare cost per year of ownership instead: purchase + support + operational hours + what it costs to change when the business changes. That last term is what nobody prices and everybody pays.

7/ Two questions that separate bids fast:
• What did you assume that, if wrong, becomes a change order?
• Who specifically is doing this, and what else are they on?

8/ And plainly: we lose bids on price. Not a complaint. Buyers optimize the number they were handed — the fix is asking for the figure that includes the decade.

9/ You don't buy a network once. You buy it every year for ten years. #ITStrategy #NetOps
