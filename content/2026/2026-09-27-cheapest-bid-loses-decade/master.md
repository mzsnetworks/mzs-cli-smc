The cheapest bid usually wins the project and loses the decade.

We're not arguing for expensive. We're arguing that the number on the comparison sheet is the wrong number, and everybody involved knows it by year three.

Here's what a low bid concretely buys you. Fewer discovery hours, so the surprises arrive later as change orders. No line item for documentation, because documentation is the easiest thing to cut and the hardest thing to notice missing. No knowledge transfer, so the environment leaves with the people who built it. Equipment chosen on unit price rather than on its support model and its lifecycle. And a scope written tightly enough that anything real is outside it.

None of that is dishonest. It's a rational response to being compared on one figure.

The costs are real. They land later, on a different budget — usually on the operations team, who never saw the bid and spend eight years explaining why a change takes three weeks.

The number worth comparing is cost per year of ownership across the expected life: purchase, plus support, plus the operational hours it will consume, plus what it will cost to change when the business changes. That last term is the one nobody prices and everybody pays.

Two questions separate bids faster than anything else we know:

What did you assume that, if wrong, becomes a change order?

Who specifically is doing this work, and what else are they on?

Both are uncomfortable to answer honestly. That's the point — the discomfort is the information.

We should say plainly: we lose bids on price, and this isn't a complaint about that. Buyers optimize the number they were handed. The fix isn't for vendors to be nobler — it's for the request to ask for the figure that includes the decade, so the honest bid and the thin one stop looking the same on a page.

You don't buy a network once. You buy it every year for ten years.

What's the last project where the cheap option turned out expensive — and how long did it take to find out?
