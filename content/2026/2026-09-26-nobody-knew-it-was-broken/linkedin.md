# LinkedIn — nobody-knew-it-was-broken


What's the last thing you fixed that nobody ever knew was broken?

I ask because I think that list is where most of the real work lives, and it's the list nobody can see — including, often, the person who made it.

The shape is always the same. You're in a device for an unrelated reason and you notice something that hasn't bitten yet. A redundant path that's been down for weeks, so the redundancy is decorative. A backup that's been running successfully against a target that no longer exists. A certificate with six weeks left on a service nobody has a calendar entry for.

You fix it in ten minutes. Nothing happens. Nothing continues to happen. That's the whole outcome.

And here's the uncomfortable part: the fix produces no evidence. There's no ticket, no incident, no thank-you, no line in a report. If you'd left it alone, there would eventually have been all four — plus a postmortem where you'd have looked competent for finding it fast.

That's not a complaint about recognition. It's structural, and it explains behavior I've seen in a lot of teams. Prevention is invisible by construction. Firefighting is visible by construction. If you only reward what you can see, you are quietly funding the second one.

Two things genuinely help.

Write it down where somebody else will read it. Not a ticket nobody closes — a line in the weekly note. "Found the standby link down since the 3rd, fixed." It takes fifteen seconds and converts an invisible act into a visible one without inflating it.

And ask it out loud in one-to-ones: what did you fix this month that nobody noticed? People light up. They've all got a list, and most assumed nobody wanted to hear it.

The best engineers I've worked with have long versions of that list and rarely mention it unprompted. The work is real. The absence of consequence is the proof.

Nobody owns the thing that works. Which is exactly why somebody has to go looking for it.

So — what's yours?
