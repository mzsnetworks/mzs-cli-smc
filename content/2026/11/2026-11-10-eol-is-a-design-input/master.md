An end-of-life notice is a design input. By the time it reaches the budget, the architecture decision has already been made for you.

The notice arrives years ahead. Last order date, last ship date, last day of software support, last day of hardware support. Four dates in a document that goes to the person who manages contracts rather than the person who designs the network.

So nothing happens. Not through negligence — the dates are distant and the platform works. The review that should have started is a conversation nobody has standing to call.

Then it becomes a budget line, and the shape of the problem has changed completely. The question is no longer "what should this part of the network look like in three years." It is "what can we buy that drops into the same slots, in this fiscal year, without a redesign." Those produce different answers, and the second one is almost always the same vendor and the same topology with faster parts.

Which may be right. But it was never decided. It was arrived at by waiting.

What I do now is unglamorous. When the notice lands, write one paragraph: what this platform does for us, what we would do differently if choosing today, and what would have to be true to do that. File it with the dates. Twenty minutes, and it is the whole difference between a refresh and a replacement.

The last-software-support date matters more than the hardware one, and it is the one people miss. A device that still forwards packets but cannot take a security patch is not supported in any way the business will accept when somebody asks.

An EOL notice is the vendor telling you when your options expire. The architecture decision happens whether or not you are in the room.

When did you last read an EOL notice as a design document?
